**Chapter 1: The Ticket**

The morning sunlight filtered warmly through the curtains of the
family\'s apartment in Pune. The comforting aroma of freshly brewed
ginger chai drifted from the kitchen, momentarily masking the unspoken
tension in the dining room. Kusha, who had recently secured a job at
Lokhandi Dhatu, sat quietly at the dining table, his gaze fixed on the
untouched cup before him.

His mother, Kavya, noticed his distraction immediately. For her,
unconditional love often manifested as relentless worry, and she had
never fully grown comfortable with her son\'s extraordinary reality. She
treated him with the same fussing affection she always had, believing
that normalcy would keep him grounded.

\"Drink your tea before it gets cold, Kusha,\" she urged, her voice
carrying that familiar, protective edge.

He blinked, pulling himself back from a heavy train of thought. \"Hmm?\"

\"The tea,\" she repeated softly.

\"Oh.\" He finally picked up the cup, letting the warmth seep into his
palms.

Kavya\'s eyes narrowed slightly, her deep empathy and strong intuition
flaring up. To an outsider, there was nothing visibly wrong with Kusha.
However, mothers naturally notice the subtle shifts in their children,
especially mothers who had spent over two decades raising a son who
occasionally woke up possessing knowledge of things he had no ordinary
way of knowing.

\"Did you have a bad dream?\" she asked quietly, her stomach tightening
with the familiar anxiety that accompanied these moments.

Kusha looked at her for a long moment before diverting his eyes.
\"Maybe,\" he replied smoothly, offering a practiced deflection.

Across the table, Sanjay, Kusha\'s father, methodically folded his
morning newspaper. As a practical man who approached every situation
with logic and calm restraint, he deliberately chose not to press for
details. He had learned long ago that if his son wanted to share a
burden, he would do so willingly. If Kusha chose silence, Sanjay knew
that absolutely no force on Earth could compel him to speak.

\"What kind of dream was it?\" Kavya pressed, her overprotective nature
overriding her husband\'s silent caution.

Taking a slow sip of his tea, Kusha offered a vague response. \"It was
nothing important\".

The answer failed to convince anyone in the room, least of all Sanjay,
who briefly studied his son\'s face before retreating behind the
reassuring barrier of his newspaper. Kusha deeply appreciated his
father\'s respect for his boundaries, just as he understood his
mother\'s complete lack of them.

\"Kusha,\" Kavya insisted.

\"Hmm?\"

\"Tell me the truth.\"

\"It was really just a dream,\" he maintained.

\"That is exactly what you said right before the water tank burst,\"
Kavya pointed out accusingly. Sanjay hid a small, knowing smile behind
his paper, while Kavya continued listing her evidence. \"And that is
what you said before that electrical fire. And before the incident with
Sharma uncle\".

\"Mom\...\"

\"That last one certainly wasn\'t a dream,\" she added triumphantly.

Deciding that finishing his tea was the safest strategy, Kusha abandoned
the argument entirely.

Fortunately, a familiar, lively voice interrupted the interrogation.
\"Kusha!\" called out his elder sister, Aparajita. She emerged from the
guest room hauling a half-zipped suitcase, preparing to return to her
husband and children in Ahmedabad. She was a few years older than him,
naturally curious, and had always been openly fascinated by his
abilities.

\"Did you book the ticket?\" she asked briskly.

\"Yes,\" Kusha replied calmly.

\"Is it confirmed?\"

\"Yes.\"

\"Lower berth?\"

\"Yes.\"

\"Air conditioned?\"

\"Yes.\"

Aparajita nodded approvingly at first, but then a frown crept onto her
face. Her eyes narrowed with affectionate skepticism, a trait she
constantly employed to tease information out of him. \"Wait a minute,\"
she said, studying him closely. \"Why are you answering my questions so
quickly?\"

\"Because you asked them,\" Kusha replied, his voice perfectly steady.

\"No, something is definitely wrong,\" she declared, planting her hands
on her hips.

Kusha instinctively looked toward his father for support, but Sanjay
immediately feigned a deep, sudden interest in an article about
municipal taxes.

Aparajita folded her arms confidently. \"What happened?\"

\"Nothing,\" Kusha said.

\"You are a liar.\"

\"Probably.\"

\"See?\" she pointed triumphantly toward their parents. \"He is
definitely hiding something\".

Kavya did not disagree, and neither did Sanjay. That was the distinct
disadvantage of growing up surrounded by a family who knew the truth;
they had collectively become experts at detecting his nonsense. Kusha
had just unwittingly triggered every single one of their internal
alarms.

Behind his calm exterior, the images from his dream remained
frighteningly vivid. He had seen a dark, unforgiving sky pouring heavy
rain, accompanied by the horrific sound of screaming metal. He had felt
the violent jolt of the derailment, seeing passengers thrown violently
from their seats amidst shattering glass and twisted iron tracks. The
overwhelming panic had eventually faded into a deafening silence. He had
awakened well before dawn, his heart racing against his ribs. More
importantly, the specific number of the train had burned itself into his
memory---it was the exact same train Aparajita was scheduled to board.

For Kusha, this was not a mere possibility or an anxious guess; it was
an absolute certainty. Over the years, his exceptional patience and
strategic mind had helped him categorize his visions, and he knew how to
distinguish ordinary nightmares from genuine premonitions. This event
firmly belonged in the latter category.

Knowing this, he had already quietly cancelled her ticket without
seeking permission, initiating a discussion, or offering any
explanation. As someone who avoided making decisions while emotions were
in control, he reasoned logically that enduring his sister\'s inevitable
anger would be significantly easier than attending her funeral.

By the afternoon, Aparajita was finally ready to depart. Her husband had
called three times to check on her progress, and her children had called
twice. True to form, both conversations with her kids had ended with
strict demands that she bring back chocolates. Taking charge, Kusha
carried her heavy suitcase down to the car.

\"You know,\" Aparajita remarked as she settled comfortably into the
passenger seat, \"normal brothers do not look quite this suspicious when
dropping their sisters off\".

\"It is a good thing that I am not normal, then,\" Kusha replied evenly.

The words slipped out before his usual restraint could catch them.
Aparajita immediately pivoted in her seat to face him. \"What exactly
does that mean?\" she demanded.

\"It means nothing,\" he deflected seamlessly.

\"That is exactly what a suspicious person would say,\" she noted
sharply.

Kusha simply started the engine, allowing the hum of the car to
temporarily drown out the conversation. Unfortunately for him, the
reprieve was brief. Halfway on their route to the airport, her curiosity
flared again.

\"You cancelled my train ticket, didn\'t you?\" she asked suddenly.

Kusha kept his eyes firmly fixed on the Pune traffic ahead. \"Yes, I
did\".

She sat up completely straight, her shock palpable. \"What? You
cancelled my confirmed ticket? When did you do this?\"

\"This morning,\" he answered plainly.

\"Without even asking me?\"

\"Yes.\"

\"Are you completely insane?\"

\"Possibly,\" he admitted mildly.

\"Kusha!\" she exclaimed in exasperation.

He pulled the car up to a busy traffic signal and brought it to a smooth
halt as the light turned red, surrounded by a gathering swarm of
vehicles. Finally, he turned his head and looked directly at her.
\"Trust me,\" he said softly.

The simplicity of his request only irritated her further. \"That is not
an explanation,\" she argued.

\"I know it isn\'t.\"

\"Then explain it to me.\"

\"I can\'t,\" he replied.

\"You won\'t,\" she corrected him.

He briefly considered correcting her assumption but quickly decided
against it, knowing that some profound truths sound inherently
ridiculous when spoken aloud, while others sound incredibly dangerous.

The traffic signal shifted to green, and the car merged forward into the
flow of traffic. Neither of them spoke for several long minutes, the
silence stretching taut between them until Aparajita finally let out a
heavy sigh.

\"You had one of those dreams again, didn\'t you?\" she asked quietly.

Kusha remained completely silent, knowing that his lack of denial served
as a definitive answer. Aparajita turned her gaze out the window,
watching the city blur past. Having grown up beside him, she had
witnessed too many impossible coincidences and strange predictions to
dismiss his actions. Still, this specific incident felt remarkably
different. The look on his face wasn\'t one of generalized anxiety or
fear; it was anchored in cold certainty. And in her experience with her
brother, his absolute certainty was always the most frightening part.

Upon arriving at the airport entrance, Kusha stepped out and unloaded
her luggage from the trunk. Aparajita accepted the handle of her
suitcase, lingering for a moment.

\"You know,\" she said softly, \"one day you are going to have to tell
me everything.\"

\"Maybe,\" he offered with a slight smile.

\"But not today?\"

\"No, not today\".

She rolled her eyes dramatically. \"You are incredibly annoying.\"

\"That is exactly what sisters are meant for.\"

\"I am the sister in this scenario,\" she reminded him.

\"Exactly,\" he countered, prompting her to laugh despite her lingering
frustration. She stepped forward and hugged him tightly. For that brief
second, her teasing, older-sister façade melted away entirely, leaving
only genuine concern. \"Take good care of yourself, Kusha,\" she
whispered.

\"You take care too,\" he replied.

She stepped back, studying his face with affectionate skepticism. \"You
always know so much more than you ever say,\" she observed.

\"I know,\" he smiled.

\"See? That is precisely the problem,\" she sighed, shaking her head as
he laughed softly. She turned and began walking toward the bustling
terminal entrance, but paused after a few steps to glance back. Kusha
was still standing near the car, watching her carefully, as if he needed
to physically verify that she entered the safe confines of the airport
building. Only when she had completely disappeared through the sliding
glass doors did he finally return to his car and drive away.

The following morning, Aparajita was in her Ahmedabad apartment, busy
helping her young daughter finish a bowl of breakfast cereal, when her
mobile phone vibrated forcefully against the table. It was a breaking
news notification. She casually unlocked the screen and glanced at the
headline, only to freeze completely. The metal spoon slipped from her
trembling fingers and clattered onto the floor.

Her daughter looked up in confusion. \"Mama?\"

Aparajita could not find the voice to answer her.

She rapidly tapped the notification, bringing up the live broadcast. The
news anchor\'s voice filled the room, reporting that a major passenger
train had derailed during the middle of the night, sending several heavy
coaches violently off the tracks. Emergency rescue operations were
currently underway at the disaster site. Then, the graphic on the screen
displayed the train number.

It was the exact same number. The very train she had originally planned
to board. The train for which Kusha had inexplicably, stubbornly
cancelled her ticket just twenty-four hours prior.

A sharp, paralyzing chill crawled its way down her spine. For the first
time in many years, the realization of her brother\'s abilities made her
feel genuinely, deeply afraid. She was not simply afraid of the horrific
accident itself, but of the staggering implications of his foresight.
Her fingers shook visibly as she navigated to her contacts list and
tapped the only name that mattered. The line rang twice before he
answered.

\"Hello?\" Kusha answered, his tone sounding infuriatingly normal and
calm.

\"Did you know?\" she demanded, bypassing any greeting.

A heavy, dangerous silence stretched across the line.

\"Kusha,\" she pressed, her voice breaking slightly.

Finally, he exhaled softly. \"I had genuinely hoped that I was wrong
this time,\" he admitted.

Aparajita slowly lowered herself into a nearby dining chair, her heart
hammering wildly against her ribs. \"When did you know?\"

\"The night before you left,\" he replied.

\"You actually saw the crash happen?\" she whispered.

Another stretch of silence followed. He offered no clear confirmation,
nor did he provide a denial. It was the exact same evasive tactic he had
utilized their entire lives, yet suddenly, his silence felt incredibly
heavy and burdened.

\"Kusha\...\" Her voice softened considerably, shifting from accusation
to profound wonder. \"Just how much do you actually know?\"

For the very first time in their conversation, Kusha did not provide an
immediate, calculated response. When he finally spoke, his voice was
hushed and reflective. \"I know much less than people think I do,\" he
murmured.

Without missing a beat, he smoothly changed the subject, inquiring about
his niece and nephew, asking if her flight had been comfortable, and
checking whether she had managed to eat her breakfast. The phone call
concluded a few minutes later, wrapped in the comforting illusion of
mundane family banter.

Long after the screen went dark, Aparajita remained seated, staring
blankly at the ongoing news coverage on the television. Throughout her
life, she had constantly wondered whether her younger brother truly
possessed some kind of strange, supernatural gift. However, as she
watched the rescue crews navigating the twisted metal wreckage, her
internal dialogue shifted drastically. She no longer questioned *if* he
knew things; the evidence was undeniable. The new question forming in
her mind was far more unsettling.

How often did he know these things?

More importantly, she wondered just how many terrible disasters had
simply never occurred because Kusha had quietly and methodically moved
someone out of their destructive path.

That profound thought lingered with her for the remainder of the day,
shadowing her every action. And for the first time in her life, she was
struck by the humbling realization that she might never truly, fully
comprehend the depths of her younger brother\'s mind.
